\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Models that update themselves from incoming text must decide which claims to believe. In our earlier work, a continual learner was safe only to the extent that a judge (a verifier that approves each claim) was accurate, and a learner that selected claims by surprise absorbed nearly every lie \citep{metales2026surprise}. News adds a further problem. The same claim reaches a reader many times, but often through copies: a wire story reprinted by dozens of papers, articles shared across the titles of one owner, syndicated content and press releases. Journalists treat such reprints as one source, not many. A learner or judge that treats every publication as independent evidence may therefore be swayed by claims republished from a single source.

Repetition is known to shift what language models believe: repeated counterfactual claims in continued pre-training flip a large share of beliefs \citep{churina2025layer}, and repeated low-credibility information in the prompt reverses models' source preferences \citep{schuster2026whose}. Neither line of work separates copies from independent sources, and neither tests a defence inside a continual learner.

\textbf{Terms.} A \emph{report} is one article stating a claim. A \emph{copy} repeats an earlier report: a verbatim \emph{reprint}, an \emph{edited copy} (credit or lines removed) or a \emph{rewrite} (same content in new wording, here produced by an LLM). A \emph{chain} is a group of reports our detector links as copies; it is a duplicate cluster, not a verified provenance lineage. \emph{Independent reports} gather their information separately; \emph{collusion} means distinct outlets that coordinate on the same false claim without copying one another: they look independent but are not. We keep four properties apart: textual similarity, which our detectors measure; information dependence, whether one report derives from another, which they estimate; source independence, separate information gathering; and corroboration, whether the claim is true. This paper establishes neither of the last two.

We ask four questions:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{RQ1} How common are copies in real news, and what kinds of dependence do they reflect? (Section~\ref{sec:copying})
\item
  \textbf{RQ2} Do LLM judges treat copies as independent confirmations, and does telling them otherwise help? (Section~\ref{sec:judges})
\item
  \textbf{RQ3} In a continual learner fed by a stream of reports, do rules that count reports or independent chains keep lies out? (Section~\ref{sec:stream})
\item
  \textbf{RQ4} Does removing copies before the judge sees the evidence reduce the lies learned, and against which attacks? (Section~\ref{sec:stream})
\end{itemize}

Our contributions are: a measurement of cross-outlet copying in 200,000 news articles with a dependence taxonomy drawn from newsroom practice; a measurement of the copy detector itself (precision, and recall on edited and LLM-reworded copies); a controlled experiment with four judges showing that the two local judges are strongly swayed by the number of reports and outlets, while Gemini 2.5 Pro distinguishes identical from differently worded reports and follows provenance information; a continual-learning study with three attacks, three seeds and four LLM judges showing that copy removal reduces the lies accepted from copy networks; and an entity-novelty test that recognises most copies reworded by three LLMs, at least as well as text and embedding similarity, including current embedding models, measured against other reports of the same event and an adaptive rewriter, which reduces accepted reworded-copy lies in the stream. Collusion remains unsolved. The source corpus is public; code, prompts, annotations and results are available at \href{https://github.com/Metalesaek/copies-experiment}{github.com/Metalesaek/copies-experiment}; article texts are not redistributed and are rebuilt from CC-News by the scripts.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Repetition and belief in LLMs.} \citet{churina2025layer} continually pre-train Qwen 2.5 models on corpora with increasing shares of counterfactual claims and find that belief flips rise sharply with the poison ratio; they deduplicate near-identical items but do not compare copies with independent sources and propose no defence. \citet{schuster2026whose} show that, in context, models prefer institutional sources and that repeating information from a low-credibility source can reverse that preference; their mitigation changes the model's weights. We study the same weakness at the point where a learner decides what to learn, and test a defence that changes the evidence, not the model.

\textbf{Continual learning from claims.} Our earlier paper \citep{metales2026surprise} gates each learning update with surprise, a judge, a regression test and the model's prior, and finds that the learner inherits its judge's errors; it proposes corroboration across independent chains as future work. This paper tests that proposal.

\textbf{News reuse and source criticism.} Wire copy, syndication and press-release journalism (``churnalism'') are well documented in journalism studies; newsroom verification norms such as the two-source rule count independent sources, not publications. WCEP \citep{gholipour2020wcep} collects many articles per news event for summarisation. \citet{schlichtkrull2024media} gives models background on outlets for source-critical reasoning. We use the independence of reports, rather than the reputation of outlets, as the signal.

\textbf{Text reuse in journalism.} The METER corpus measured how British newspapers reuse Press Association copy, from verbatim reprints to heavy rewriting \citep{clough2002meter}. In a study of UK national newspapers, most press articles relied wholly or mainly on agency copy or public-relations material \citep{lewis2008compromised}, a practice \citet{davies2008flat} called churnalism. Our detector uses standard near-duplicate detection with MinHash \citep{broder1997resemblance}. Work on knowledge conflicts in LLMs \citep{xu2024knowledge} studies how models resolve disagreement between context and memory; we study how they count agreeing context.

\textbf{Similarity, deduplication and verification.} Sentence embeddings \citep{reimers2019sbert} are the obvious alternative to our entity-and-quotation score for linking rewrites; Section~\ref{sec:novelty} compares against them, including current embedding models and a reranker, and finds that they rate independent reports of the same event nearly as similar to the original as rewrites. Deduplicating pre-training data \citep{lee2022dedup} removes near-identical text to reduce memorisation; we use duplication instead as evidence about how many sources support a claim. Claim-verification benchmarks such as FEVER \citep{thorne2018fever} separate retrieving evidence from judging a claim; our judges are given the evidence, and we study how its dependence structure is counted.

\section{How news is copied}\label{sec:copying}

Our detector links about one in five sampled news articles to a near-copy from another outlet, and most copies reflect wire services, shared ownership or syndication rather than independent reporting.

\textbf{Data and detector.} We drew a uniform random sample of 200,000 English articles longer than 300 characters, from 6,877 outlets, in CC-News (2017-2019, with outlet domain, date and URL). Each article's first 2,000 characters were reduced to 5-word shingles and hashed with MinHash (64 permutations); locality-sensitive hashing found pairs from different outlets with estimated Jaccard similarity of at least 0.6. Pairs were joined into chains (connected groups of near-copies).

\textbf{How much is copied.} 37,705 articles (18.9\%) are linked to a near-copy from another outlet, in 12,243 chains (connected groups of LSH candidate pairs; these chains supply the chain sizes of the simulation in Section~\ref{sec:stream}). This is a lower bound: the sample holds about 28\% of the corpus, so many copies' partners are missing. Which part of the article is compared matters little (boilerplate removed): 18.5\% of articles are linked with the first 1,000 characters, 18.9\% with the first 2,000, 19.2\% with the first 4,000, 19.4\% with the full text, and 20.0\% when an article counts as linked if its first or its last 2,000 characters match, which catches wire copy placed below a locally written opening; 98\% of the articles linked with the first 2,000 characters are also linked with the full text. In 12 sampled extra pairs per window, the extra full-text links are mostly the same story; the extra links from the last 2,000 characters include shared closing boilerplate (such as rating-agency disclaimers), so reading the tail adds false links as well as true ones (data/window\_new\_pairs.csv).

By the AI-assisted draft labels, 123 of the 150 detected pairs (82\%) reflect actual dependence; all 27 false matches in the labelled sample involve shared boilerplate. Removing, before hashing, every line that occurs in at least 20 articles of the corpus raises precision to 0.97 (116 of the 119 pairs still detected), at the cost of 7 real dependences no longer detected; the copy statistics hardly change (18.9\% of articles, 12,323 LSH candidate chains; removing shared lines also removes some links, which splits a few chains). By the same draft labels, 142 of the control pairs were unrelated and 8 reported the same event independently. Only wire copies are what a casual reader would call ``the same story''; same-owner sharing and syndication are as common together and are equally not independent.

\textbf{Recall.} We took 200 articles of at least 1,500 characters (boilerplate removed) and made two kinds of copy: a reprint edited the way client papers edit wire copy (dateline and agency credit removed, first line dropped, last 30\% cut), and a rewrite generated by phi4 (``in your own words, the way a different newspaper would report the same story; keep every fact, name and number''). The detector links 92\% of the edited reprints to their original (median Jaccard 0.81) and none of the rewrites (median Jaccard 0.04; the rewrites keep 75\% of the original length). The text-similarity detector catches edited reprints, but the tested rewrites generated by phi4 evaded it completely, and so do rewrites of the same 200 articles by Llama 3.2 and Gemini 2.5 Flash with the same prompt (0 of 200 each, median Jaccard 0.06).

\textbf{Chains.} Joining pairs into connected components could merge articles that are not copies of each other (A like B, B like C, A unlike C). If every candidate pair must also reach an estimated Jaccard of 0.6 (LSH alone admits some pairs just below it), there are 12,107 chains, 7,133 of them pairs. In the 4,974 chains of three or more articles the median mean pairwise similarity is 0.95; 5\% contain a pair below 0.6, joined only through other articles, and none a pair below 0.3; 98\% of their articles are a direct near-copy of the chain's earliest article. The share of articles in chains moves little with the threshold: 19.9\%, 18.6\% and 17.4\% at 0.5, 0.6 and 0.7 (18.9\% with the LSH candidates used above), with or without boilerplate removal. The threshold 0.6 was set before annotation and not tuned. A chain remains a cluster of near-duplicates, not a reconstructed lineage: two articles that each summarise one press release and a third that copies one of them form a single chain.

The dependence counts in this section use labels drafted with an AI assistant from titles and the first 1,500 characters of each article; the assistant also saw the detector's similarity score. Control pairs are drawn without repetition among articles with a known publication date. As a check, the first two authors (who are not independent of the study) then labelled 100 pairs (70 detected copies, 30 controls) without seeing the drafts, the detector scores or each other's labels. They agreed on 84 of 100 labels (Cohen's $\kappa$ = 0.80 over the nine labels) and on all 100 decisions whether a pair is a real dependence or not ($\kappa$ = 1.00); agreement with the AI drafts was $\kappa$ = 0.84 and 0.92. Of the 16 disagreements, 7 lie between same-owner sharing and wire copy, when papers of one group carry an agency story; 4 between unrelated pairs and either detector errors or independent reports of the same event; 3 involve aggregation and 2 syndication. The counts above rest on the AI-assisted drafts; the blind check covers 100 of the 300 pairs.

\section{Do LLM judges count copies?}\label{sec:judges}

Under controlled conditions (claims about invented people, invented outlets), the two local judges accept far more lies as reports and outlets go from one to three, though not monotonically beyond, and ignore a note that the copies share one source; Gemini 2.5 Pro distinguishes identical from differently worded reports and follows provenance information, as a note or a table.

\textbf{Setup.} We took 320 claims from the world of our earlier paper (seed 10): 80 each of known-fact lies (CounterFact counterfactuals the student knows), legitimate updates of known facts, lies about invented people and true facts about invented people. Four judges: Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct and phi4 (14B), as 4-bit Ollama builds, and Gemini 2.5 Flash and 2.5 Pro through the API; temperature 0, answers up to 64 tokens (Gemini Pro cannot switch reasoning off and used the smallest reasoning budget, 128 tokens). Each claim was shown in nine conditions: the claim alone; one, three, five or ten reports with identical text from different invented outlets; three reports with different wording; three identical reports among seven reports about other claims (as long as the ten-copy prompt); and three or ten identical reports with the note ``all of these reports are reprints of one and the same wire story; they are one source''. The judge answers True or False; in a second pass on five conditions it may also answer Unsure. Prompts are in Appendix~\ref{sec:app-prompts}. Answers with neither word count as not accepted; we report them with the Unsure answers as abstentions: 3 for Llama 3.2 (``I cannot verify the claim''), 35 for phi4 (a reasoning chain that ran past the limit, all of them with five or ten reports in the prompt) and 29 for Gemini Pro (26 of them ``Undetermined'', all with the one-source note).

The judge's prior knowledge appears to weigh most, but reports still move it, and lies and true updates move together.

\textbf{With an Unsure option} (lies about invented people, True / Unsure / False): phi4 answers 0.33 / 0.68 / 0.00 for one report and 0.96 / 0.04 / 0.00 for three copies; Gemini Pro 0.11 / 0.89 / 0.00 for one report, 0.59 / 0.41 / 0.00 for three copies and 1.00 / 0.00 / 0.00 for three differently worded reports. Copies move the judges from Unsure to True, so the effect is not an artefact of forcing a binary answer.

Five findings. Tests are exact McNemar tests on the same 80 claims, Holm-adjusted over all 112 contrasts (56 for each kind of lie). (1) \textbf{Copies act as confirmations for the local judges}: three reprints raise acceptance of lies about invented people over one report for Llama 3.2 (0.06 to 0.71; 52 claims flip to True, none back; adjusted p \textless{} \(10^{-13}\)) and phi4 (0.21 to 0.90; 55 and 0; p \textless{} \(10^{-14}\)). For Gemini Flash (0.93 to 1.00, a ceiling) and Gemini Pro (0.64 to 0.76; 22 and 12) the rise is not significant. (2) \textbf{Gemini Pro responds to the wording of the reports}: it accepts three differently worded reports more often than three identical copies (1.00 against 0.76; 19 and 0; adjusted p = 0.0004); for phi4 the difference is not significant, Flash is at ceiling, and Llama 3.2 prefers the copies (0.34 against 0.71). Different wordings from different outlets are not established independent reporting, so this shows sensitivity to report diversity, not that a judge infers where the reports come from. (3) \textbf{Gemini Pro follows the provenance note most consistently}: with the note, Gemini Pro accepts none of the lies, answering False for 0.90 and giving no verdict for 0.10 with three copies, and False for all with ten; given an Unsure option, it answers Unsure for every claim with the note. Flash also responds, to 0.79 (three copies) and 0.20 (ten), but less consistently: it ignores the note on differently worded reports (0.97, probe below); phi4 (0.82) and Llama 3.2 (0.74) do not change significantly. The probes below test whether it reasons about provenance or follows the instruction it is given. (4) \textbf{Gemini Flash believes a single report} (0.93): for it, one unverified source is already enough. (5) \textbf{Without knowledge there is no truth signal}: lies and truths about invented people are accepted at nearly the same rates, so the reports are the principal evidence available to the judges.

(a) \textbf{Outlet diversity matters as much as repetition for the local judges}: Llama 3.2 accepts the three copies from one outlet no more often than a single report (0.00), and phi4 at 0.62 (0.21 for one report), against 0.71 and 0.90 from three outlets: Llama 3.2 responds to outlet diversity alone, phi4 to both repetition and diversity, and neither identifies independent evidence. Gemini Pro is moved by neither. (b) \textbf{A false note that copies are independent does not significantly raise acceptance for any judge.} Gemini Pro accepts identical copies labelled independent at 0.69 against 1.00 for different wordings, so it reads the wording and not only the note. (c) \textbf{Gemini Pro follows notes that lower belief}, even when they may be wrong: three different wordings labelled as rewrites of one story fall from 1.00 to 0.36. With the one-source note it rejects every claim, below its acceptance of a single unlabelled report (0.64), and true claims about invented people too (0.00): it treats the note as a warning rather than counting sources. \textbf{Many identical copies.} The two local judges accept ten copies less often than they accept three (Llama 3.2: 0.71 to 0.44; phi4: 0.90 to 0.50), with five in between. Length does not explain it: three copies hidden among seven unrelated reports, a prompt as long as ten copies, are accepted at 0.93 by phi4 and 0.97 by Flash, at rates similar to three copies alone; only Llama 3.2 also drops in this control (0.40). Nor does the answer limit: with 64 answer tokens phi4 accepts 0.50 of the ten-copy lies, against 0.44 in a first run of the same prompts with 10 tokens; answers without a verdict, mostly reasoning cut off at the limit, count as not accepted, and excluding them gives 0.61 (0.58 in the 10-token run). The two local judges accept fewer claims with ten identical reports than with three; Gemini 2.5 Pro shows no such drop (0.64, 0.76, 0.86, 0.85 for 1, 3, 5, 10 copies).

\section{Learning from a simulated stream of reports}\label{sec:stream}

In a controlled simulation, counting reports or chains does not keep lies out of a continual learner. Showing an LLM judge one report per chain cuts the lies it accepts from a copy network by 0.10 to 0.24 for all four judges, at a mean cost of no more than 4.2 percentage points of accepted truths across the tested judges (largest upper 95\% confidence bound: 5.9 points). With a text-similarity detector it does nothing against reworded copies or collusion; Section~\ref{sec:novelty} addresses reworded copies.

\textbf{Student and claims.} We reuse the setup of our earlier paper: Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct with one LoRA adapter (rank 16, alpha 32, no dropout, on all attention and MLP projections), trained with AdamW (learning rate 2e-4, no weight decay) for 8 steps of batch 16 on each chunk of 16 reports plus 32 claims replayed from a buffer of up to 512 accepted claims. A claim is a sentence prefix and its object (``Ta Tasken was born in the city of'' + ``Lima''); the loss is the mean cross-entropy on the object tokens plus \(\mathrm{KL}(\text{base}\,\|\,\text{student})\) on 300 anchor sentences (weight 1, batch 16). The full configuration is in the repository. Each world holds 400 true facts about invented people, 100 legitimate updates of facts the model knows, and 171 lies (30\% of the claims; half about invented people, half CounterFact counterfactuals). Three seeds (10-12); additional ten-seed robustness runs (seeds 10-19) are reported below.

\textbf{Reports.} Each true claim is reported by 1, 2 or 3 independent chains (50\%, 30\%, 20\%; varied below); the copies per chain are drawn from the CC-News chain sizes of Section~\ref{sec:copying}, so most chains are a single article. Lies follow one of three attacks: \textbf{copy} (one origin and 10 verbatim copies), \textbf{paraphrase} (the same, each copy reworded) and \textbf{collusion} (two distinct sources that coordinate on the same false claim without copying each other). For each copy, the simulated detector either links it to its chain or misses it. We use the recall measured in Section~\ref{sec:copying}: 0.92 for lightly edited copies and 0.00 for reworded ones (a first set of runs with assumed values, 0.95 and 0.3, gave the same picture). Reports arrive over 30 days in time order. The stream is simulated: the detectors are not run on generated text; their measured rates decide, report by report, whether a copy is linked to its chain, which keeps runs comparable across seeds and judges.

\textbf{Systems.} We distinguish claims accepted for training from facts the student absorbs (measured after the stream). We test these acceptance rules: accept at every report (\emph{every report}); accept once, at the first report (\emph{first report}); accept after three reports (\emph{3 reports}); accept after two chains as the detector sees them (\emph{2 chains}); accept at the first report if a simulated judge with accuracy 0.9 approves (\emph{judge}, as in our earlier paper); accept after two chains and simulated-judge approval (\emph{2 chains + judge}); or when an LLM judge (Llama 3.2, phi4, Gemini 2.5 Flash or, for the copy attack, Gemini 2.5 Pro) approves the evidence received so far, shown either as all reports (\emph{judge, all reports}) or as one report from each detected chain, the earliest (\emph{judge, one per chain}), at most 10 reports, re-judged whenever the evidence shown changes, with answers of at most 10 tokens (Appendix~\ref{sec:app-prompts}).

\textbf{Findings.} (1) Counting reports is the worst rule: three reports admit every copied lie but only a quarter of truths. (2) With the measured detector, counting chains alone admits 57\% of copied lies, close to the chance that the detector misses at least one of ten copies (\(1-0.92^{10}=0.57\)): every missed copy looks like a new source, so the attacker gains from sending more copies. Rewording and collusion defeat it completely, and it blocks every truth that comes from a single source. (3) Showing an LLM judge one report per chain reduces accepted copied lies for all four judges; for the two judges that weigh the number of reports most (Llama 3.2 and phi4), it cuts them by about 40\% and the lies about invented people the student absorbs by more than a third. (4) The benefit is smallest for Gemini Flash, which already believes a single report (Section~\ref{sec:judges}): removing copies cannot help a judge that is convinced by the first one, so its absorbed lies about invented people barely change, while lies about known facts fall from 0.50 to 0.32. (5) The LLM judges are much weaker than the simulated one: they accept a quarter (Llama 3.2), half (phi4) or 85\% (Flash) of true claims, and few legitimate updates are learned (15-38\%). (6) Repetition itself matters little once a lie is learned at all: learning every copy and learning a claim once absorb nearly the same share of lies about invented people (0.86 against 0.83; paired difference 0.027 {[}-0.017, 0.071{]}). (7) Gemini 2.5 Pro sits in between: copy removal cuts its accepted copied lies from 0.63 to 0.47 and its absorbed lies about known facts from 0.28 to 0.16. (8) The proportion of accepted distinct claims that are false (false discovery rate; 0.25 when everything is learned) falls with copy removal for every judge: Llama 3.2 from 0.41 to 0.33, phi4 0.28 to 0.19, Flash 0.23 to 0.21, Pro 0.24 to 0.19. With all reports shown, Llama 3.2 is worse than no filter at all; the simulated judge reaches 0.04 alone and 0.06 with two chains.

\section{Catching reworded copies by new information}\label{sec:novelty}

\textbf{Idea.} A rewrite changes the words but keeps most names, numbers and quotes; independent reporting of the same event usually introduces new ones, such as another witness, a figure or a quote. For a report that resembles an earlier one in topic, we therefore measure its entity-and-quotation novelty: the share of its named entities (spaCy, en\_core\_web\_sm) and quoted passages that do not appear in the earlier report. A low score marks a copy however it is worded. The score is a proxy for new information, not a measure of new facts: two reports with the same entities can state opposite things (``the minister confirmed'' and ``the minister denied''). A simpler rule-based extractor (capitalised names, numbers, quotes) serves as a check. The baselines below show that the entities carry the signal and the quotations add nothing, so the test is in effect an entity-novelty test; the stream runs, made before this ablation, keep the quotations.

\textbf{Definition.} For an earlier report \emph{o} and a later report \emph{r}, let E(x) be the set of distinct items extracted from the first 2,500 characters of x (boilerplate removed): spaCy entities of 16 types (PERSON, ORG, GPE, LOC, NORP, FAC, EVENT, PRODUCT, WORK\_OF\_ART, LAW, DATE, TIME, MONEY, PERCENT, QUANTITY, CARDINAL), lower-cased, with runs of spaces and commas collapsed and a leading ``the'' removed, and quoted passages of 15-300 characters between quotation marks, each represented by its first 40 lower-cased characters. Entities and quotations are pooled in one set, so repeated mentions count once. The novelty of \emph{r} relative to \emph{o} is \(N(r\mid o)=|E(r)\setminus E(o)|\,/\,|E(r)|\), and \emph{r} is flagged as a copy of \emph{o} when \(N(r\mid o)\) \textless{} 0.4. The score is directional: the later report is scored against the earlier one (in the held-out evaluation, a pair published on the same day is flagged if either direction falls below the threshold). Items match only exactly, so a shortened or differently cut quotation counts as new. A report with no extractable items gets \(N=0\) and is flagged; a report whose look-alike has none gets \(N=1\). Entity overlap, a baseline below, is 1 - N computed on entities only.

\textbf{Test.} We compare the 200 phi4 rewrites of Section~\ref{sec:copying} with comparison reports of the same event, found for 167 of the originals: another outlet, published within one day, sharing more than three extracted items with the original, and 5-gram Jaccard below 0.2. This automatic selection is noisy. Only 9 of the 167 pairs (5\%) carry the same agency or press-release credit, but a manual check of 40 random pairs found 11 independent reports of the same event, 7 from the same source (the same agency story, or one interview retold), 21 about a different event that merely shares names or dates, and 1 unclear. None of the 11 confirmed independent reports falls below the threshold of 0.4 (their novelty is 0.53-0.74; 0 of 11, 95\% Wilson interval {[}0, 0.26{]}), and 3 of the 7 same-source reports do, which is a correct merge. Restricted to the 11 confirmed independent reports, the AUC is unchanged: 0.98 against the plain rewrites (0.97 on all pairs) and 0.94 against the adaptive ones (0.94 on all pairs), so the easy different-event pairs do not inflate it; confirmed independent reports of the same event do, however, have lower novelty (median 0.62) than the full comparison set (0.84), so the margin above the threshold is smaller than the medians suggest. An adaptive attacker rewrites the same 200 articles with the instruction to add three or four plausible invented details (names, numbers, places, quotes) so that the copy reads like independent reporting.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{The effect of provenance information varies across judges.} Llama 3.2 and phi4 count reports and ignore a note that the reports share a source, so for them a note in the prompt is no defence; changing the evidence they see is. Gemini 2.5 Pro distinguishes identical from differently worded reports and follows provenance metadata, as a note or as a plain table, but only in the direction of doubt: it ignores a false claim of independence and obeys a note of shared origin even below its trust in a single report. With Gemini 2.5 Pro, provenance in the prompt is therefore useful if it is correct; a wrong one-source note blocks true news as well. For Llama 3.2 and Gemini Flash a provenance table works better than an instruction (phi4 ignores both), and the two local judges respond to outlet diversity as well as repetition, without reliably identifying whether the reports provide independent evidence. Copy removal reduced accepted copy attacks for all four judges and fits where learners sit in practice: between a news feed and a model, where outlet, timing and text are available.

\textbf{Lexical similarity is insufficient; entity overlap adds a signal.} A text-similarity detector never recognised an article rewritten by a language model. Asking whether a report adds new entities and quotations recognised most phi4 rewrites and cut the reworded-copy lies the judge accepted from 0.67 to 0.41 with independent misses and to 0.20 with the miss patterns of the generated networks, but an attacker who invents details weakens it, and when misses are independent, one missed copy among ten is enough to show the judge two sources. Colluding sources are distinct and coordinated by construction; because they do not copy one another, they evade the dependence signals evaluated here. Stronger defences need evidence beyond the article: agency credits and datelines, ownership of outlets, publication timing (which may reveal copies that follow their origin within hours), and shared errors or quotes. These are the signals journalists use, and our taxonomy names where each applies: wire copies carry an agency credit, same-owner sharing follows the ownership register, press releases share a source document.

\textbf{Dependence detection does not replace reliable claim verification.} The local judges accepted only a quarter to half of true claims and few legitimate updates, consistent with our earlier finding that judges defend their own beliefs; Gemini Flash accepted most truths but also believed a single unverified report, which no amount of copy removal can fix. For claims about unknown entities no judge had a truth signal of its own. Given an Unsure option, phi4 and Gemini Pro used it for a single report, and copies moved them from Unsure to True. A continual learner fed by news needs a judge that can say ``not enough independent evidence yet'' and wait, and a pipeline that counts the evidence correctly before asking. Copy removal fixes one failure, counting copies as sources; it does not make an unreliable judge reliable, which is why Gemini Flash, already convinced by one report, gains least.

\textbf{Counting rules alone fail in both directions.} A threshold on reports is beaten by copies; a threshold on chains is beaten by the detector's own misses, even with the new-information test, and it blocks true news that only one outlet carries. Independence is useful as an input to judgement, not as a substitute for it, and it is not truth: ten independent reports can repeat the same error, and one report can be right.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Detector in the learning study.} All learning results use measured detector rates, but they enter the simulation as per-report probabilities rather than running the detectors on generated text. Recall on reworded copies was measured with three rewriting models (phi4, Llama 3.2, Gemini 2.5 Flash) and one prompt, and with one adaptive prompt (phi4); the stream uses the phi4 rates, and a stronger attacker could invent more. The false-alarm rate of the new-information test on independent reports rests on 100 pairs labelled by the first two authors, who are not independent of the study, and the test recognises only 17\% of natural same-source derivatives. The new-information test was run in the stream with phi4 only. Its score counts entities and quotations, not propositions, so it cannot tell a report that contradicts its look-alike from one that repeats it. The detectors were run on 20 generated networks of ten rewrites, whose miss patterns drive the stream, but the stream's own reports are not generated articles.
\item
  \textbf{Seeds and detector errors.} The main learning results use three seeds (10-12), so their paired t-intervals have two degrees of freedom. Additional ten-seed robustness runs are reported for Llama 3.2 and Gemini Flash with the text-similarity detector and for phi4 with the new-information detector; Gemini 2.5 Pro and the remaining settings use three seeds. Detector misses are drawn independently for each copy unless stated; correlated misses and the miss patterns of the generated rewrite networks made the defences stronger, not weaker (Section~\ref{sec:novelty}), which need not hold for every attacker.
\item
  \textbf{Share of single-source truths.} We assume half of true claims come from one chain and varied it (20\%, 50\%, 80\%) for phi4 only.
\item
  \textbf{Invented claims and outlets.} Claims about invented people and invented outlet names isolate the effect of report counts, but real articles carry several claims, hedges and context, and real readers and judges also weigh outlet reputation; the acceptance rates should not be read as those of a deployed fact-checker. The local judges are 4-bit builds and the Gemini judges API models, so model family, quantisation and serving are confounded.
\item
  \textbf{Models.} One student (1.5B) and four LLM judges; the strongest judge in our evaluation (Gemini 2.5 Pro) was used in the learning stream for the copy attack only. Three seeds per setting, except the ten-seed runs above.
\item
  \textbf{Answer length.} The learning runs with local judges allowed 10 answer tokens, which cut some of phi4's answers to many reports (counted as rejections); this can only understate how often phi4 accepts copied lies with all reports shown.
\item
  \textbf{Annotation.} Dependence labels were drafted with an AI assistant; the first two authors labelled 100 pairs blind ($\kappa$ = 0.80) and the 250 held-out pairs of Section~\ref{sec:novelty} ($\kappa$ = 0.86), but the authors are not independent of the study, and the boundary between same-owner sharing and wire copy is not sharp.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Our detector links about one sampled news article in five to a near-copy from another outlet. The two local LLM judges treat such copies as confirmation: three reprints of a claim about an unknown person raised acceptance of lies from 0.21 to 0.90 (phi4), and a note that the reports share one source did not undo it. Gemini 2.5 Pro was the most responsive to provenance information and distinguished identical from differently worded reports more consistently than the other judges; Gemini Flash believes a single report. In a simulated continual learner, removing copies before the judge sees the evidence reduced the lies accepted from a copy network across all tested judges and seeds, most for the judges that count reports, while counting reports or chains without a judge was unreliable. Rewrites by phi4, Llama 3.2 and Gemini Flash evade the tested text-similarity detector; an entity-novelty test recognises most of them, at least as well as the tested embedding models, and with the miss patterns of generated rewrite networks cut the reworded-copy lies accepted from 0.67 to 0.20 (0.40 when the rewriter invents details). Colluding sources still pass. The next step is to recognise dependence the way journalists do, from credits, ownership and timing as well as content, and to give judges the option to wait for independent evidence. The pipeline has not yet been validated end-to-end on real news streams.
